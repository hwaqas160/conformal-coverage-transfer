1. Use **natural, clear academic English**. Make the writing sound like a researcher explaining the work to other researchers, not overly polished or formulaic.

2. Prefer **short and medium-length sentences**. Break unnecessarily long or multi-clause sentences into two or more sentences, while naturally varying sentence length.

3. Avoid excessive **em dashes (—), double hyphens (--), semicolons, colons, and parentheses**. Prefer straightforward sentence structures.

4. Remove **unnecessary commas**, especially before “and” or “or” when grammatically unnecessary. Use the Oxford comma only when it improves clarity or follows the journal style.

5. Avoid repetitive sentence openings and structures. Do not repeatedly use phrases such as **“This study,” “The proposed method,” “Furthermore,” “Moreover,”** or **“Additionally.”**

6. Prefer **simple, precise vocabulary** over unnecessarily sophisticated wording. Use “use” instead of “utilize” when both have the same meaning.

7. Remove **filler, redundancy, and generic AI-style phrases** such as “it is worth noting that,” “it should be noted that,” “leveraging the power of,” “paves the way,” and “holds great promise.”

8. Use **active voice where natural**, while retaining passive voice when appropriate for scientific writing.

9. Keep the tone **objective, precise, and moderately confident**. Avoid exaggerated or promotional terms unless they are objectively justified.

10. Preserve **all scientific meaning and technical details**, including terminology, equations, model names, datasets, numerical values, statistical results, citations, and important qualifications.

11. **Do not mechanically paraphrase every sentence.** Keep sentences that are already clear and natural. Rewrite only where necessary to improve clarity, flow, and readability.

12. Do not intentionally introduce mistakes or awkward wording. The goal is **natural human academic writing, not artificial imperfections**. Preserve the author's voice and scientific reasoning while improving the writing.
